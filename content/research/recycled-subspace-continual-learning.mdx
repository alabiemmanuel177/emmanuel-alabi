## The conflict

Two lines of work each solve half of continual learning and break the other
half.

**Continual Backprop** keeps a network learning indefinitely by continually
reinitialising low-utility units. It never saturates, it never goes rigid, and
it stores no data. It also forgets: nothing protects what earlier tasks built,
so a reset is free to destroy it.

**Subspace methods** — GPM, and TRGP for transfer — do the opposite. They
record the directions earlier tasks relied on and project new gradients away
from them. Old tasks survive. But the protected subspace only ever grows, the
space of permitted updates only ever shrinks, and the network eventually
cannot learn at all.

Grafting one onto the other naively fails in a specific way, and the failure is
the interesting part: reinitialisation and projection are *fighting over the
same resource*. The reset mechanism does not know which units carry protected
structure, so it destroys exactly what the projection is trying to preserve.

## The idea being tested

**One budget, two worlds.** Units are recycled at a constant rate; directions
are evicted when the subspace budget is full; and the same budget governs both.
Three couplings are what make that more than a bolt-on:

1. **Unit → direction.** A memory-aware utility makes reinitialisation defer to
   protected directions, so a reset stops destroying retained structure.
2. **Direction → unit.** Recycled placement makes new units grow *out of* the
   protected subspace rather than beside it, which is where transfer should
   come from if it comes from anywhere.
3. **Budget coupling.** A single recycling rate governs both, under a memory
   ledger that stays flat as tasks accumulate — no stored samples, ever.

Whether those couplings do anything is precisely what is unresolved.

## Predictions, written first

The design fixes a stage ladder and states, in advance, what each stage should
do to a five-criterion report card — retention, memory growth, plasticity,
freedom from task labels, and forward transfer.

| Stage | Added mechanism | Prediction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S0 | Continual Backprop baseline | Reproduces the published behaviour: plasticity holds, retention fails |
| S1 | Protected subspace + projection | Retention rises and **plasticity collapses** — the tension, reproduced inside the method |
| S2 | Memory-aware utility | Retention restored, plasticity partially recovered |
| S3 | Budgeted eviction and recycling | **Plasticity returns while retention holds** — the core claim |
| S4 | Trust-region transfer | Transfer appears without costing retention |
| S5 | Recycled reinitialisation placement | Transfer improves further |

S1 is a prediction that the method will get *worse*, and S3 is the one the whole
idea stands or falls on. Stating both in advance is the point: a ladder written
after the fact can be made to fit whatever happened.

## What exists

Implemented and running end to end: permuted-MNIST and split-MNIST streams, an
MLP exposing per-layer activations, the Continual Backprop utility and
reinitialisation path, the subspace memory with online second-moment estimation,
budgeted selection with lease carry, projection with a transfer gate, and the
metrics — task accuracy, effective feature rank, dead-unit fraction, prototype
bank, memory ledger.

**Not established:** anything at all about whether the couplings work. The runs
to date are single-seed and hand-tuned while the mechanisms were being
calibrated. They told me the basis timescale matters and that protecting a
classifier head is useless if the features underneath it drift — both of which
are facts about the testbed, not findings about the method.

<Note title="Why no numbers appear here">
It would be easy to post the most encouraging calibration run. But a single seed
chosen after looking at several is not evidence, and the prediction ladder above
only means something if the numbers that test it are produced under a frozen
configuration and multiple seeds, after the predictions were fixed. Results will
appear here when that has happened, including if they contradict the ladder.
</Note>

## Where it connects

Research 1 to 3 asked how a robot should represent and act on uncertainty that
already exists. This asks a question underneath that: what a network may safely
*change about itself* while it keeps learning, and what it must hold still.

That is the same question Research 4 was scaffolded around — what may adapt
online and what must stay fixed — approached from the learning-algorithm side
rather than the navigation stack. The testbeds here are deliberately small and
standard, because the mechanism has to be shown to work somewhere clean before
it is worth putting near a robot.
